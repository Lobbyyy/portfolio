I was in a bit of a rut because I had not received an investment from Antler. Being so close to achieving my goals, yet so far, really messed with my head.

I started applying for jobs in sales, startups, venture building, venture capital, and investment banking.

This year, I promised myself I'd run 100km every month. It was something that I'd never done before, but it seemed achievable. I observed David Goggins running Ultras and 100 miles in a day. If David could do that, and he's twice my age, surely I could do 100km in 30 days. How hard could it be?

In January, I went on 17 runs, covered 92.79 km and spent 9:26:30 hours running. This experience made me realise that running 100km was within my reach.

After Antler, I needed to build a source of revenue. I had the inkling to start a sales automation agency. It would be a hybrid model — a mix of Consulting and SaaS. I'd craft the Go-to-Market strategy and leverage SaaS tools to execute the plan. Many of the Founders in my network had no idea how to grow and scale their business, so I thought my services could bring them value.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!F4X2!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3f188b80-7058-457e-b032-d3fcd276fa41_1900x392.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!F4X2!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F3f188b80-7058-457e-b032-d3fcd276fa41_1900x392.png)## February

I started Outbound Hero. I know LinkedIn like the back of my hand, and with this knowledge I helped a few pre-seed and seed stage companies develop their Sales and Go-to-Market strategy. I laid the foundations for market entry into their designated verticals. 

Continued applying for jobs. I started to get interviews, but the positions could have been more desirable. I did the interviews anyway.

In February, I went on 13 runs, covered 96.29 km and spent 9:10:48 hours running. I decreased the frequency of my runs and increased the volume per run.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!e9dv!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc7f39134-7cc6-4729-b959-fe6f4d0417e2_1900x392.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!e9dv!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc7f39134-7cc6-4729-b959-fe6f4d0417e2_1900x392.png)## March

Continued applying for jobs. The count was up to 250+ applications at this point. I began to despise ATS systems and just the general hiring process. I got tired of constantly having to sell myself.

I got hit by a car near Waterloo as I was cycling. I was fine. I had minor injuries, but my bike can't say the same. I want to say that my life flashed before my eyes, but it didn't. The only flash was that of a blue BMW. 

In March, I went on 15 runs, hit 100.6 km, and spent 8:42:18 hours running. When I hit 100 km, I was over the moon. I was getting faster and looking great. Running became so enjoyable that I started doing recovery runs (running to recover from running sounds counterintuitive, but I loved it). 

I sunset Outbound Hero. I enjoyed working with Founders, but the agency model was unscalable. It required more face time than I had anticipated. It was skewed to consulting rather than SaaS. In addition, it was giving me a negative ROI. My clients wanted my help, but they couldn't afford my services. If I wanted to go for clients with more money (Series A and beyond), I'd need to bolster my services, which would entail even more face time. I could foresee my trajectory on this path, and it didn't correlate with what I wanted for my life.

I went back to the drawing board to think of software ideas. After Outbound Hero, I wanted to build a pure SaaS business. Antler had given me a great framework to think of ideas and problems, so I revisited this and started to generate a few ideas. I validated them with industry experts. The idea took some shape. I called it 6 degrees.

I gave up the job search. I was in late-stage interviews with a few companies I wanted to work for, but they didn't materialise. I took a good, hard look in the mirror and realised why I was unsuccessful in my job search. I'd spent my professional life optimising to be a startup founder ([why startup founders should be more like thanos](https://deckandadream.substack.com/p/what-thanos-and-i-have-in-common)),  and the hiring manager in these companies didn’t care about diversity of experiences. They wanted someone that would fit into a nice little box. Someone who would just put their head down and do what they are told. Unfortunately, that's not me. 

I made entrepreneurship my only focus.

6 degrees gained even more shape. I scrapped a Proof of Concept (PoC) together using no-code tools. 

Antler had concluded its 11th cohort, and I knew there would be some disgruntled Founders in the same position as myself in January— founders who didn't receive investment but wanted to work on a startup. I didn't care that Antler didn’t invest in them; after all, they didn't invest in me, and I'm talented, skilled, and hungry.

I met two founders who were particularly interesting as they were working on a very similar idea. We decided to join forces and collaborate together. It was a collaboration that made a lot of sense. One Founder had a sales background, and the other had a technical background. When we decided to collaborate, my idea was much more mature than theirs. They knew what they wanted to do but didn't know how. I knew how.

It took courage for me to collaborate with them. I was protective of my idea and had the leverage because I knew how to execute it. I was also aware they had leverage. While I knew how to do it, they were in a much better position as a team to execute it (they could build an actual tech product - not just a scrappy no-code PoC). If the collaboration didn't work, I'd have shown my cards and showed them how to do it, and then they could take those cards and stack the deck against me. After a few chats with each Founder, I made a judgement call. I let down my walls and showed them my cards. I realised the success of this collaboration depended on trust and transparency.

We divvied positions, and I became the Chief Product Officer (CPO). I was okay with this role, as I had a clear vision for the product. I trusted the CEO's ability to sell and the CTO's ability to develop the product. Together, we were a fantastic team on paper.

The momentum I felt in my professional life translated to the treadmill. I went on 17 runs, accumulated 110.5 km and spent 11:29:00 hours running.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!PHJ-!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff593ceaa-cccd-4074-865c-647d8878c8bc_1900x392.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!PHJ-!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Ff593ceaa-cccd-4074-865c-647d8878c8bc_1900x392.png)## May

Working as part of a team with the CEO and CTO was enjoyable. After months of grinding on my own, it felt good to be a part of a team. They were also great individuals, which helped.

However, I noticed cracks starting to form. We'd collaborated for over three weeks and were still in 'discussion mode'. Comparing this to the progress I'd made on my own raised a red flag. In the short time I worked on the idea, I'd already stitched together a PoC. I hoped working with two additional individuals on the same mission would triple the productivity. Instead, it divided productivity by three.

We struggled to align on a plan and then execute it. This was frustrating because we were such a strong team, and I had complete faith that we could raise funding with this team composition.

On 10th May, in our morning meeting, I highlighted my concerns and decided to part ways. I couldn't wait for 'things to click'. I have been in many co-founding relationships, and when I observe red flags, I don't wait for things to go wrong. It was a tough conversation. I knew I'd already shown my cards, but I didn't care. I liked them as people and genuinely wanted them to succeed. Let the best team (or solo Founder) win.

After the co-founder breakup, I was on my own again. There is no other way to say it — I felt shit.

I wasn't looking for a rebound. I couldn't be asked to meet with other Founders to see if there were opportunities for collaboration. I've done that roadshow so many times, and it hasn't worked. Insanity is doing the same things, expecting a different result.

I switched gears. I set the goal of developing the Minimum Viable Product (MVP) myself. If I could get to an MVP, I knew I could attract users and raise capital with this. Along this journey, I knew I'd attract developers and potentially even a technical co-founder.

The challenge was figuring out how to translate the PoC into an MVP. A few years ago, I developed a sales enablement tool using a no-code tool (Bubble.io), but 6 Degrees was much more technical. For the app to function as I envisioned, I needed to process thousands of data points. This fell into the data science domain, and I needed to figure out how to use Python.

On 20th May at 8:20pm, I sat in my dining room and opened Visual Studio (VS) Code for the first time. I had no clue what I was looking at.

I can't describe how humbling that experience was. Here I was: an LSE graduate with over half a decade of professional work and life experience, but l felt like a toddler looking at my laptop.

There were so many windows within this one window, and I didn't know what they did. Terminal, Ports, Explorer … I'm sorry, what?! Where do I write the code? Where do I upload files? What do I do to see it on my browser?

By 9:10 pm, I wanted to close my laptop and call it a day. I remember the feeling so vividly. My whole body was itching to stop. I wanted to do anything else except for this. But I sat my ass down on that chair and didn't move. I clicked every button on the interface, watched YouTube videos, and bombarded GPT with questions.

By 4:07 am on 21st May, I'd written my first bit of code in Python and was processing some data. I know the timings to the letter because I recorded videos throughout that night. Watching my code process data changed me. It changed the boundaries of possibilities. I realised that what I thought was my global maximum was, in fact, my local maximum. I was thinking too small and promised never to make that mistake again.

My running really took a hit from the co-founder breakup. By 14th May, I only ran 25.34 km. I returned on the 23rd and tried to recuperate as much as possible. I ran 40 km that week, bringing my total to 65.34 km. 

I only ran 8 times that month. I felt terrible for not keeping the promise I made to myself. I re-evaluated whether I wanted it and how badly I wanted it. I told myself that I wouldn't let this happen again.

In June, I reaffirmed my promise. I ran 16 times, covered 100 km and spent 10:27:21 hours running. It felt great.

I spent the first half of June talking to GPT. I'd tell GPT what I wanted, get the code, put it into VS code, test it out, and then repeat—over and over and over and over again. I pulled a few all-nighters doing this. There was something so addicting about it.

I have many developer friends, and I understand why they love coding. There is something inherently attractive about being locked in, writing code, testing it out, and then repeating. It's just you and your program. It reminded me of the time I studied Art for GCSE. You get lost in the flow, and you flow.

While I was making progress, this experience was extremely frustrating. GPT isn't perfect, and bits of code were deleted whenever I asked for revisions. As a noob developer, I didn't know what the code was doing. I would copy and paste the code into VS Code like a mindless monkey. I would test these revisions only to realise they fixed a few things but broke something else. I'd then go back to GPT, asking it to fix the broken bits of code, and then it would break something else.

I did this over and over and over again and over again.

It was so jarring because you'd make so much progress, but then one ambiguous prompt would ruin everything. At this point, I had no idea how to use Github, so once the code was ruined, I binned everything and started over again. It's akin to creating a fabulous-looking Word document only to not have saved it. You lose the work, and you lose your progress.

It got so frustrating that there were a few points where I started to give GPT prompts in caps locks. "FIX THIS PLS"; "WHAT ARE YOU DOING?"; "YOU'RE NOT LISTENING TO ME". I don't know what I hoped to achieve with this because I was talking to a robot, but I was running out of options. Trust me, when it is 5:30 am, and you've spent the whole day and night going back and forth, only to have your work destroyed, it can take you to some dark places.

By the end of June, I snapped. I needed to readjust my approach because I didn't want to be #1 on GPT's kill list once AI took over, and I'd probably lose my sanity before GPT got to that stage.

I realised my frustrations weren't with GPT; they were with myself. I needed to stop being a mindless monkey and brute-forcing my way to an MVP. I needed to understand what the code was doing. I couldn't rely on GPT as a crutch; I needed to use my mind and leverage GPT as a means to an end. I couldn't be led by GPT; I needed to lead it. 

I started Harvard's CS50—Introduction to Computer Science course to achieve this.

The course was 11 weeks long, and I enjoyed it tremendously. As someone who loves tech products, I've only been able to experience them as users. Looking behind the curtain to see how tech products are built was enlightening. I was shocked to realise that they are just a bunch of 0s and 1s.

The first few weeks of the course were tough, but it felt great to be learning again.

I initially thought I'd start by watching the videos, making notes, and then restarting my idea. This felt efficient, but after completing the first three weeks of the curriculum, I realised that I was taking the easy way out. The whole point of undertaking this course was to understand programming, not remember programming.

I restarted the course and did all the weekly exercises, and I'm so glad I did. I noticed a vast chasm between theory and application. I understood all the theory in the lectures; it made sense. But it was a whole different beast when I tried to do the weekly exercises. With time, I started to think like a computer, which made all the difference.

I went for 18 runs, covered 101.7 km and ran for 10:16:06 hours. This was my second consecutive 100 km month, and I felt proud.

At this point, I started recording content. I loved how far I had come in the last six months and wanted to document the journey. I recorded myself learning, running, and gym sessions—basically everything I wanted to remember. I wanted a keepsake—something I could reflect on when I 'made it' to see how far I had come.

I also realised that I needed to make the journey more fun. The perk of working for yourself is the autonomy it provides. I was not limited to a cubicle, an office, or any particular area. I could get work done as long as I had my laptop and Wi-Fi. I worked in the park, art galleries, coffee shops, hotel lobbies, restaurants, public libraries, and anywhere else that my mind could conceive. My imagination and bank account were the only blockers.

I made the tough decision to move back home. It was a big decision for me because I loved living with my friends, and I didn't know how moving back home would affect my mental health.

As a young adult, you spread your wings and fly out of the nest. Having to come back home felt like someone had clipped my wings. Is a bird without wings still a bird?

Like Icarus, I'd flown too close to the Sun, and my wings had melted.

However, I didn't have a choice. After my collaboration with the two founders fell through, so did my fundraising prospect in Q2/3. As a result, I needed to drastically financially re-engineer my life to reduce my burn rate and extend my runway. I did this in a few ways:

I cancelled all my subscriptions. Bye: Class Pass, Deliveroo, Apple TV, Now TV, Disney +, Crunchyroll, LinkedIn Premium, Hinge, Brilliant, Duolingo, Strava, Blinkist, Pret, PureGym, and much more. I also cancelled all my business subscriptions, such as Bubble, Framer, Tilebit, etc.

I replaced steaks, Greek salads, and meals out with meal deals, Magi noodles, and food from the discounted section in the Co-op. My diet during this phase was wild. I added the most random things to Magi noodles to 'spice it up'— eggs, lettuce, beans, spam, different noodles, etc. Every day was an adventure in the discounted section, and I'd time my visits to the Co-op for the optimal Calories: taste: price ratio. FYI, 2pm and 5:30pm are the best times.

I stopped dating. As much as my limbic system craved some human connection, it just didn't make sense. Talking stages took time, and dating took energy—both of which were in low supply in this phase of my life.

Despite these sacrifices, I needed more time and had to move back home.

[![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qn0h!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F627c0705-7a0e-42bb-9c72-1d887814cbf5_1900x392.png)](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!qn0h!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F627c0705-7a0e-42bb-9c72-1d887814cbf5_1900x392.png)## August

My first month back home was surprisingly good. When I lived out, I came home every two weeks, so seeing my parents and little brother every morning was a pleasant change.

Before I moved out, I used to get into arguments with my parents quite a lot. I lived at home during my second and third year at university, and during this period, I wanted to live life. I wanted to explore and do things for the first time, and I wanted to party. I couldn't explain this to my parents, who wished to know every detail of my activities. This caused a chasm between us, and it was one of my motivations for moving out in the first place.

Moving back, I thought it would be the same, but it wasn't. I immediately noticed the space they gave me, and I appreciated this. I felt their support and unconditional love. I do not have the words to describe how much their support means to me. Thank you, Mum. Thank you, Dad.

My first month at home also made me realise I was worrying about nothing. My mental health didn't digress. Instead, it made me stronger. Reconnecting with my parents and learning about their lives and daily day-to-day activities awoke parts of me that were dormant for so long.

Not having to worry about my finances or what I would eat was an enormous weight off my shoulders. After months of scavenging in the discount section, waking up to a fridge full of food brought me so much happiness. Instead of struggling to survive, I began to thrive. I suddenly had so much more energy. It put me in a state of play.

This was also the month that I got introduced to Pieter Levels. I observed how he had built his life, and this was the closest approximation to what I wanted. This was great as it gave me a north star to aim towards.

By mid-August, I completed the CS50 course. For my final submission, I created an app called Sands. I made Sands to help people live mindful, intentional, and purposeful lives. Time is the most scarce resource, and Sand's Visual Time Calendar empowers users to understand this. 

I was eager to test this newfound programming knowledge, so instead of building my idea in March, I created something much more straightforward to get my feet wet. It was a habit activity monitor called Twelve. This time around, I actually understood what the code was doing. I understood the structure and its purpose. The syntax was still alien, but I didn't obsess over this.

For three weeks, I talked to GPT every day for 10 hours, repeating the same process I had done in June. This time, I could clearly see where the issues were arising and address them before pasting them into VS code. This saved me a lot of time and headaches. I built the front-end and the back-end, but connecting them with APIs became a significant blocker.

I built and rebuilt this app four times, trying different methods, but by the end of the third week, I snapped again for the second time this year. I lost my mind. I was so frustrated. I went to the park and started punching the ground with unbounded rage. I sat in the spinning bowl in the kids' park for an hour, trying to cool down.

I had learnt so much, but it still wasn't enough. When was enough, enough?

The course was great, but it was an introduction to computer science. If I wanted to build a production-ready application, I needed to know more. I needed to understand more.

I readjusted my approach.

I talked to my developer friends and asked them how they learnt to code. One of them pointed me to some tutorials on YouTube. Unlike the computer science course, these tutorial walked me through building production-ready applications. There were tutorials on everything, including creating a Figma clone, Notion clone, Netflix clone, etc. This was a fantastic revelation, and I planned to go through these tutorials.

I went for 20 runs, covered 107.6 km and spent 9:56:27 running.

I spent September following these tutorials and building applications. Each tutorial was 5-10 hours long. I made a list of 30 applications I wanted to build and set the goal of creating 30 applications in 30 days. I called this challenge 30 for 30. I wanted to learn, and I wanted to do it as fast as possible. If I completed this challenge, I would have built a clone of every big tech company that exists. Surely, this would be enough?

It's funny how optimistic and wrong I was.

My first production-ready app was Creatorr. It generated conversations, code, music, images, and videos using OpenAI and Replicate APIs. I started the app on 4th September, and it took me 15 days to complete.

Halfway through the month, I realised there was no way I'd get 30 apps built in 30 days. There were a few things I didn't account for:

In hindsight, it sounds like an obvious oversight, but I didn't account for the time I'd have to write the code. I was under the impression that if the video segment was 10 minutes long, I could watch it at 2x speed and write code in 10 minutes. Oh, how wrong I was. I didn’t track my exact timings for my first app, but a 5-hour video took 15 days to complete. On average, it took 30x longer.

Updated libraries: This was a less obvious oversight. The tutorials were good because they used the latest technologies, and much of the nitty-gritty code was abstracted away (i.e., using Clerk for Authentication instead of building it yourself). The downside for a rookie developer is that these technologies also get updated more regularly. This means libraries change and syntax is deprecated. I can't tell you how frustrating this realisation was. I wanted to sit back and emulate the tutorial but couldn't. I was forced to read through documentation (all of which I didn't understand) to try to debug my issues. Most of the build was smooth, but debugging deprecated syntax killed my productivity.

Regardless, I did get to experience a few high points during this process. I was proud that I had written out all the code myself. The sense of accomplishment and euphoria I felt when I debugged the deprecated syntax was insane. I also built my first production-ready application using Tailwind, React.js, Next.js, Prisma, and MySQL. It had everything, and it looked great.
