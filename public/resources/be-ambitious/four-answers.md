## Step 1: the basic ask

**The prompt:**

> Give me some ideas to get my first 100 customers for my new app.

**The answer:**

I don't know what your app does or who it's for, so these are general. Tell me more and I can narrow them down.

**1. Do things that don't scale (customers 1-20)**
- Make a list of 50 people who have the problem you solve, then DM or email each one personally. Offer free setup in exchange for 15 minutes of feedback.
- Ask friends, former coworkers and your network for introductions to people with the problem. Don't ask them to try the app themselves.
- Onboard each person by hand on a call. You'll learn why people sign up or bounce, and early users remember that attention.

**2. Go where your users already gather (20-60)**
- Join the subreddits, Slack and Discord groups, Facebook groups and forums your users hang out in. Answer questions for a couple of weeks first, then mention the app only where it fits. Straight self-promotion usually gets removed.
- Search X, Reddit and LinkedIn for people complaining about the problem or about a competitor. Reply with help, not a pitch.
- Find people who use your closest competitor and are unhappy. Review sites like G2, Capterra and app store reviews list them by name.

**3. Launch in public (a burst of signups)**
- Post on Product Hunt, Hacker News (Show HN), Indie Hackers and BetaList. Prepare a clear one-line pitch, a short demo video and a founder story.
- Submit to niche directories and "alternatives to X" listing sites. These get steady, low-effort traffic.

**4. Build in public**
- Share short posts or videos on your progress, what you learned and your numbers. It brings in people who want to follow along, and some become users.
- Show the app solving one specific problem in a 30-second clip. TikTok, Reels and Shorts can work well for consumer apps.

**5. Build referrals in from the start**
- Give both sides a reward, such as extra credits or a free month.
- Let free users share their output, with a small "made with [app]" badge.
- Ask every happy user for one introduction.

**6. Partner with people who already have your audience**
- Offer a free account and a custom link to newsletter writers, niche YouTubers and podcasters. Small creators with 2-10k followers are often easier to reach and convert better.
- Cross-promote with a non-competing tool that serves the same users.

**7. Make content that matches how people search**
- Write a few pages like "How to [solve problem]" and "[Competitor] alternative."
- Give away something useful for free, such as a template, calculator or checklist, with a signup behind it.

**8. Paid options (only after something converts)**
- Run a small test of $200-500 on one channel to see if people sign up. Don't scale it until you know your conversion rate.

**How to run it**
- Pick two or three channels and stick with them for a month. Spreading across all eight will dilute your effort.
- Track where each signup came from.
- Aim for about 5 new customers a week. At that pace you reach 100 in about 5 months, and launches or partnerships can speed it up.

If you tell me what the app does, who it's for, and whether it's free, paid or B2B, I'll put together a specific 30-day plan with scripts for the first outreach messages.

## Step 2: altitude

**The prompt:**

> My real goal isn't 100 customers. It's knowing within 30 days whether people will pay for my app, so I can decide whether to keep going. Design the smallest plan that gets me a clear yes or no, and tell me what result would count as each.

**The answer:**

# 30-day plan: will people pay?

I don't know what your app does or who it's for, so this plan works for any app. The test is whether strangers hand over money or a firm commitment. Compliments, signups, and "looks cool" don't count.

## The test

**Charge real money for the real thing, to a specific group of people, within the first 2 weeks.** Use a payment link (Stripe or Lemon Squeezy) that charges a real card. If the app isn't finished, sell it as a paid early-access or founding-member deal, and say honestly when it ships.

## Before day 1 (1 day)

Write these down and don't change them later:

1. **Who:** one narrow group you can reach directly, such as a subreddit, a Slack or Discord community, or your own contacts in that niche. Don't pick "everyone."
2. **Price:** the actual price you'd need to build a business. Don't test a low price you can't live on.
3. **Thresholds:** the yes and no lines below, set before you see any data.

## Weeks 1–2: get the offer in front of 150–300 right people

- Build a one-page site: the problem, what the app does, the price, and a Buy button that charges.
- Put it in front of your target group by direct outreach. Send 100+ personal DMs or emails, and post in 2–3 communities where those people already gather. Don't rely on ads or SEO, because they're too slow for 30 days.
- Spend an equal amount of time on 10 short calls with people who replied. Ask what they do today about the problem, what it costs them, and whether they've paid for anything like it. Don't pitch on these calls.

## Weeks 3–4: close and measure

- Follow up with everyone who showed interest. Offer a founding-member discount if you need one, but still take payment.
- If you have a working product, let a few paying or committed people use it. Check whether they come back, since that tells you whether you'd keep customers.

## What counts as yes or no

Adjust these numbers to your price and niche. The structure is what matters.

| Result by day 30 | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **5+ strangers pay** (not friends or family), or 10+ give a card-backed pre-order | **Yes. Keep going.** |
| 1–4 strangers pay, **or** many say "I'd pay" but few do | **Maybe. Fix one thing and run 2 more weeks.** Change the price, the audience, or the pitch, but only one. |
| **0 strangers pay** after 150+ qualified people saw the offer, and the calls show no pain they currently pay to solve | **No. Stop or pivot.** |

Two caveats:

- **A no only counts if enough of the right people saw the offer.** If only 20 people saw it, you learned about your outreach, not your app. In that case, extend the test.
- **Friends, family, and "I'd definitely buy it" don't count.** Only completed payments, or pre-orders with a card, count.

## What to skip

Skip polishing the app, building features, a logo, a launch on Product Hunt, ads, and any free tier. None of them tell you whether people will pay.

If you tell me what the app does and who it's for, I can write the landing page copy, the outreach message, the call script, and set the thresholds for your actual price.

## Step 3: depth

**The prompt:**

> My real goal is knowing within 30 days whether people will pay for my app, so I can decide whether to keep going. Do this the way the best early-stage founders would. Before you plan anything: tell me what raw material they would start from (not my opinion of my idea), what methods they would use, and what evidence other than you or me would prove the answer. Then ask me for that raw material, one question at a time.

**The answer:**

**Raw material the best founders start from**

They don't start with your description of the idea. They start with things that exist independent of you:

1. **A named list of real prospective buyers.** Actual people or companies you can reach this week, not a "target market."
2. **What those people do and spend today.** Their current workaround, the money or hours it costs them, and what they've already paid for something similar.
3. **Behavior data you already have.** If the app is live, that means usage, signups, and any payments. It also includes past conversations, emails, and waitlist or traffic numbers.
4. **The competitive record.** Competitors' pricing pages and reviews, and complaints in forums. If nobody pays for anything nearby, that's a finding.

**Methods they'd use in 30 days**

- **Past-behavior interviews.** Ask "tell me about the last time this happened and what you did," not "would you use this?" (the Mom Test).
- **Asking for money early.** Pre-sales, paid pilots, or deposits, with a real price.
- **A price-bearing page plus direct outreach** to the list from #1, measuring how many take a costly action.
- **Doing it by hand for a few people** (concierge), to see whether they pay and come back.
- **If the app is already live,** a conversion and retention analysis of existing users.
- **A pass/fail threshold written down before any data comes in**, for example "5 of 40 contacted prospects pay by day 30." This stops us from rationalizing afterward.

**Evidence that isn't you or me**

- Strangers' money: charged cards, paid invoices, or deposits.
- Costly commitments: a signed letter of intent with a price, a booked paid pilot, or an intro to their budget holder.
- Repeat behavior: people who come back or renew without being nudged.

Compliments, signups, "I'd totally pay for that," and my opinion of the idea don't count.

**First question**

What exists today: just an idea, a prototype, or a live app? If it's live, how many people have used it, and how many of them have paid you anything?

## Step 4: range

**The prompt:**

> My real goal is knowing within 30 days whether people will pay for my app, so I can decide whether to keep going. Do this the way the best early-stage founders would, starting from raw evidence, and judged by something other than you or me. Then pull in other fields: what would a direct-response marketer, a behavioural economist and a sceptical investor each actually do to test this (do what they do, don't write like them)? Combine it into one 30-day plan with a pass/fail line.

**The answer:**

I don't know what the app is, who it's for, or whether it exists yet, so the thresholds below are defaults. Change them on Day 0 and never after. If you tell me the app, audience, price and budget, I'll set the numbers.

## The judge

Strangers' money and usage data decide this, not your opinion or mine.
- Stripe (or app store) payouts and the ad platform's dashboards are the scoreboard.
- Friends, family and your existing audience don't count. Only cold traffic and cold outreach do.
- On Day 0, write the pass/fail line and the kill rule on one page. Send it to one person who will hold you to it. After that, the only way the number changes is by someone else's data.

## What each outside field contributes

**Direct-response marketer**
- Builds one offer, one landing page and one checkout, then sends paid cold traffic to it.
- Tests 3–4 angles (different headlines and promises for the same product) with small spend and keeps the one with the best click-through.
- Reads cost per click, visitor-to-checkout rate and cost per paid order, not "interest."

**Behavioural economist**
- Ignores what people say they'd pay and watches what they do (revealed preference).
- Randomises price across visitors, e.g. $X, 2X and 4X, to get a willingness-to-pay curve instead of a guess.
- Requires a real card charge, because a costly action is the only honest commitment signal.
- Tests annual versus monthly as the default, and uses the interview question "What did you do last time this happened, and what did it cost you?" instead of "Would you use this?"

**Sceptical investor**
- Tries to disprove it first: excludes the network, counts only strangers, and demands cohort numbers.
- Asks for payback (acquisition cost versus first-year revenue) and for retention (do people come back at day 14?).
- Runs a pre-mortem on Day 0: "It failed. Why?" Each answer becomes a metric.
- Uses the Sean Ellis question on paying users: "How would you feel if you couldn't use this?" 40% or more should say "very disappointed."

## 30-day plan

**Days 0–1: commit**
- Write the pass/fail line, the pre-mortem and the budget. Seal them with your accountability person.

**Days 2–7: raw evidence**
- Do 15–20 conversations with strangers who have the problem. Find them in communities, via cold messages, or among people already paying for a workaround.
- Record and transcribe each one. Ask about the last time the problem happened, what they did, and what they paid in money or hours.
- Log verbatim quotes in a sheet.
- Gate: at least 8 of 15 describe the problem unprompted and are already spending money or real time on a workaround. If fewer than 5 do, stop here or reframe the problem. That costs a week and nothing else.

**Days 8–14: offer and page**
- Build one landing page that uses the customers' own words from the interviews, plus a working Stripe payment link.
- If the product isn't finished, sell it honestly as a pre-order with a delivery date and a full refund. Don't imply it exists if it doesn't.
- Launch 3–4 ad angles with a small budget. Randomise the price across three tiers.

**Days 15–24: scale what works**
- Put the budget (about $500–1,000, adjust to your price) behind the winning angle and price.
- Run cold outreach in parallel. Record every payment, refund and objection.
- Contact everyone who reached checkout but didn't pay and ask why. These are your best data points.

**Days 25–30: retention and decision**
- Check whether payers are using the core action by day 14 of their own usage.
- Ask payers the Ellis question.
- Export the raw CSVs and compare them to the sealed line. The decision meeting is Day 30, with your accountability person present.

## Pass/fail line (defaults)

**Pass** needs all four:
1. At least 25 cold strangers paid. At least 10 of them paid the middle or top price tier, not just the cheapest.
2. Cost per paying customer is no more than one-third of 12-month revenue per customer.
3. At least 40% of payers did the core action 3 or more times by day 14 of their own usage.
4. At least 40% of payers answered "very disappointed" to the Ellis question.

**Fail** if any one of these holds:
- Fewer than 10 strangers paid.
- Interviews failed the Day 7 gate.
- Cost per paying customer is above first-year revenue.

**In between (10–24 payers):** you get one 14-day iteration, only if you name a specific change you expect to move a specific number. If it still isn't a pass, stop.

For a very cheap app, such as $5 a month, raise the payer counts. For a high-priced B2B tool, lower them and treat 5 paid pilots from strangers as a pass.

If you give me the app details I'll adjust the thresholds and draft the interview script, landing page copy and ad angles.
